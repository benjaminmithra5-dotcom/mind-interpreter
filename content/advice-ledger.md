# Advice ledger

Every specific suggestion, technique, script or solution each article has
already given, so no later article repeats it. Each new article adds its
own section at the end. Keep each line short.

These don't count as repeats, because every article may need them: the
crisis line, the domestic violence hotline sentence, suggesting a doctor
or mental health professional, and the link inviting the reader to a
private conversation at /consultation.

## 2026-10-05 · how-to-tell-your-partner-you-feel-lonely

- Before talking, pin down the loneliness with self-questions: when it's strongest, the last time you felt close, what exactly you miss, what you've stopped saying.
- Name the "shape" of the loneliness in one phrase ("I feel like a roommate").
- Avoid starting late at night, mid-argument, during chores or scrolling, or right after a disappointing evening.
- Give a heads-up earlier in the day and ask for time alone together that evening.
- Speak about what you feel and want, not what your partner does wrong ("I've been missing you" rather than "You never talk to me").
- Sentence template: "I feel… when… and I'd love…"
- Write a few lines down beforehand, or open with "This is hard for me to say, so bear with me."
- Bring one recent, concrete example instead of a list of grievances.
- If they get defensive: "I'm not blaming you. I'm telling you because I care about us."
- Ask them back: "Have you felt any of this too?" / "What's it been like for you lately?"
- Don't aim to solve it in one evening; aim for more understanding.
- Ten minutes a day of phone-free talk about something other than chores and schedules.
- An end-of-day question: "What was the best and hardest part of today?"
- One weekly shared activity just for the two of you (a walk, cooking, an old movie).
- Check in again after a couple of weeks: "How are we doing? Has anything felt different?"
- Notice and turn toward small bids for connection.
- If the feeling is too tangled, talk it through first with a neutral person outside the relationship.

## 2026-10-07 · cant-stop-replaying-what-you-said

- Treat the replay as a stuck rehearsal: the mind wants an ending, and each run gets harsher.
- Split replay from repair: ask "if they said 'that hurt,' would I want to respond?"
- If yes, send a short repair line (scripts: "It came out wrong. I meant…", "Can you finish what you were saying?", "That joke landed differently than I intended.").
- If no, don't send anything; a message would only feed the loop.
- Replay it once from the other person's chair and watch them, not yourself; they're busy with their own half.
- At night, jot a one-line note ("Decide tomorrow at 10 whether it needs a repair") and put the pen down.
- Write the replay out once in a single paragraph, then close the notebook.
- Choose one sentence for next time ("Let me say that a different way") instead of rewriting the past.
- Shift the body's job: wash something in hot water, walk to the end of the street, name five things you see.
- Ask the underlying question aloud: "What am I afraid this means?"
- Remember one sentence is not evidence about who you are; trust the whole record.

## 2026-10-09 · adult-child-stops-calling

- First message should show you've been thinking of them, ask for nothing, and say no reply is needed; avoid "is everything okay?" which reads like a bill.
- Scripts: a true shared memory (the family dog) with "No need to reply"; "I'm not looking for anything, I just wanted you to know I'm here"; "Whenever you feel like a call, any day, any hour, I'll pick up."
- If you suspect your part, apologize once, name it plainly, promise to just listen, then stop; no "but I was only trying to help."
- If you have no idea what you did: "I don't want to guess. If there's anything you want me to know, I'm listening."
- Match the size of a short reply with an equally short one; don't ask "are you angry with me?"
- If they ask for space: "I hear you. I love you, and the door is open." Don't ask for how long.
- Test before sending: "If they never answer this, can I still send the next one without resentment?"
- Keep a year-long rhythm: one small note every two or three weeks (garden photo, song line, game score).
- Give the waiting company: one fixed, non-negotiable thing in the week unrelated to them (a Thursday walking group, a class, a standing coffee with a friend), because waiting swells to fill empty space.
- Tell one person aloud what's happening, not for advice but to stop carrying it silently.
- If you have a real reason to think your child may be unsafe, don't wait for a reply; contact local authorities and ask for a welfare check.

## 2026-10-12 · move-back-to-your-hometown

- Say your reason in one sentence and check whether it points toward something or away from something.
- Delete the thing you're leaving (bad job, breakup) and see if you still want the hometown.
- Notice which old role (quiet one, responsible one) waits for you, and decide ahead what you won't take back on.
- Picture an ordinary Tuesday in March six months in, and name two or three actual people you'd see in the first month.
- Join one standing weekly activity the week you arrive.
- Trial-live there for ten days to two weeks as regular life (work, groceries, errands); judge by day nine, not day two.
- Alternatively rent short-term for a month, or consider a nearby town or city an hour away.
- Question the rule that a life only counts if it ends farther from where it started.
- Imagine telling the people you admire most and notice whether you're wincing at the audience, not the town.
- Drive your old school or first-job route slowly with the radio off and notice whether you feel like a visitor, a prisoner, or someone who lives there.

## 2026-10-14 · boss-gave-vague-feedback

- Pause one beat, thank them, then ask for a scene rather than a definition ("a time in the last month when you wished I'd done it differently").
- Ask "If I were doing this well, what would you see me doing?" and "Is there a meeting coming up where I could try it?"
- Say the feedback back with two guesses ("offering an opinion before the group decides, or being visible with executives?") and let them correct you.
- Ask "What would make you notice the change?" when they can't define the problem.
- In public, say "Appreciate that. Can I grab ten minutes later this week?" and book it that day; or a plain "Noted, thank you."
- Send a short next-day note naming one behavior and one date, ending "Tell me if I've misread it."
- If they dodge, narrow to a choice: "Which one thing on my plate would you most like to see handled differently?"
- After two tries: "I'm worried I'm guessing. Could we pick one thing together for the next month?"
- Treat vague criticism as small information (they want something), not a verdict on who you are.

## 2026-10-16 · empty-after-getting-what-you-wanted

- List the scaffolding the chase gave you (alarm, Sunday purpose, group chat, "working toward" identity) to turn a vague void into a specific, replaceable loss.
- Finish "When I get this, I'll finally feel ___" and find a modest version of that feeling available this week (call a friend, an hour phone-free).
- Mark the win deliberately: one hour doing something only possible because it finished.
- Tell one person who saw the cost, not just those who congratulate.
- Keep one old routine with no outcome attached (run at dawn for no reason, write one paragraph).
- Put two or three ordinary plans with people on the calendar unrelated to the win.
- Rest two to four weeks before choosing the next goal.
- Test the next goal: would you still want it if no one ever found out?

## 2026-10-19 · friends-dont-invite-me-anymore

- Invitations go to whoever comes to mind first (fast replier, nearby, free schedule), not by a vote; being forgotten is not the same as being excluded.
- Count your last five invitations; frequent "maybe" or "can't" teaches friends to stop asking.
- Attach a date to a decline: "Can't make Friday. Could we do coffee the week after?"
- Don't hunt for a cause at 2 a.m.; look only for a real shift (job, move, a joke that landed badly).
- Script: "I saw the pictures from Saturday, and I'll admit it stung a little. I'd love to be there for the next one." Ask for the next plan, not an explanation.
- Ask one safe friend privately; never in the group chat or three people in a row.
- For one month, send the invitations yourself: specific place, time and easy exit ("tacos on Elm, Thursday 6:30, come if you can"); invite one friend, not the whole group.
- Six-week test of two or three low-pressure overtures; watch whether anyone reaches back.
- If it's one-sided, stop pouring hope into that friendship without ending it, and invest in people who show up (neighbor, coworker, classmate).
- Closing image: the person holding the camera is in the party but not in the frame; take the next picture yourself.

## 2026-10-21 · not-coming-home-for-thanksgiving

- Tell parents about four weeks ahead (early November) so they have time to be sad and recover; late news only leaves time to be hurt.
- Tell a sibling the day before so they don't hear it secondhand or become the negotiator.
- Write a sticky note with one sentence (the no) and one dated yes (a December weekend, or a call during cooking).
- Keep the reason to one honest line; long explanations invite fixes like "we'll buy the ticket."
- Call rather than text; open with "I've made a decision about Thanksgiving, and I wanted you to hear it from me," then state the no and the yes, then stop.
- Avoid hedges ("I might not," "we'll see"); a maybe invites pushing, and let the silence sit.
- Ready answers to guilt lines: "We won't be here forever" → "That's why I'm planning December now"; "Your cousin is flying in" → "I'm glad she is. I still can't this year"; "Did we do something?" → "No. This is about my calendar, not about you."
- Repeat one calm sentence ("I know it's disappointing. I'm still not coming this year") instead of new arguments.
- If a parent cries, stay on the line without changing the answer, or end kindly with "Let's talk tomorrow."
- Ask "What's the part of Thursday you'd miss me at most?" and join that piece (called in during the stuffing, same recipe on video, on speaker for the thankful round).
- Plan the three o'clock hour on the day: coworker's invitation, volunteer shift, Friendsgiving potluck, or a shaped solo day (one family dish, walk before dark, saved movie); send mom a photo of your plate.

## 2026-10-23 · who-am-i-now-that-im-retired

- Reframe the hole as missing being known by the work, not missing the work; the task is new ways of being known, not a replacement job.
- Write your day as verbs, not a title; verbs come home with you and point to skills still wanting use.
- Three-part answer to "What do you do?": nod to the past, a present fact, one curiosity; practice it aloud in the car; no "I'm just retired."
- Aim to be needed by one named person (neighbor with a phone, nephew's résumé) with one skill at a regular time, not generic volunteering.
- Spend one afternoon first-name-only in a place that doesn't know your title (library workshop, pool, weekday pottery class) and see how people respond.
- Monthly lunch with former coworkers with a rule: first twenty minutes not about the office.
- Closing image: the old badge in the kitchen drawer.
