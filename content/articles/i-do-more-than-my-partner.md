It's 9:40 on a weeknight. You're wiping the counter for the second time today, and your partner is on the couch, laughing at something on their phone. Nothing is wrong, exactly. But a number is running in your head. Dinner, three. Laundry, two. The pediatrician's phone call, the dog, the birthday card for their sister. You didn't ask to keep this count, and you can't stop.

If "I feel like I do more than my partner" is what you've been typing into a search bar, I'd like to start by saying that the feeling is real information. It's also, I think, a poor accountant. Both things can be true, and the useful part is learning which is which.

## Why does the tally keep growing?

A tally grows when it has nowhere to go. Every time you do something and say nothing, the entry stays open. You're waiting for a thank-you, a "let me get that," or just a flicker that says they noticed. When it doesn't come, you don't cross the entry off. You carry it.

That's why the score often feels bigger than any single chore. The dishes aren't what stings. What stings is the quiet suspicion that the dishes are invisible, and that you are too. Notice which entries make your chest tighten and which ones you shrug off. The tight ones are rarely about effort. They're about being seen.

## Why can't they see what I'm doing?

Partly because you each count in a different currency. You count tasks: things done, things remembered, things planned before anyone asked. They may count hours at work, the traffic they sat in, the worry they never mention. Neither of you is lying. You're adding up two different ledgers and comparing the totals.

Try this once, on paper. Sit down together, each with a sheet, and write everything you believe you carry: the visible jobs, and the invisible ones, like noticing the milk is low or knowing the school's pickup time changed. Then trade sheets and read each other's. I'd bet each page holds at least one line that makes the other person say, "I didn't know that was on there." It isn't a trial. It's two maps being laid next to each other, and most couples have never seen both at once.

## How do I bring it up without starting a fight?

Choose one item, not the total. "You never help" invites a defense, and a defense ends the conversation. One item doesn't. Pick the one that stings the most and say what it costs you in plain words: "When I'm still cleaning up at ten and you're relaxing, I feel alone in the house. I'd like us to finish together."

Then say what you actually want, which is probably not an apology. It might be for them to notice, so you can try: "I don't need you to do everything. I'd like you to see it when I do." That sentence is harder to argue with than a list, because it asks for something small and true.

And own your half of the silence. If you've always handled it, they may honestly believe the arrangement suits you. "I never told you this was getting heavy" is not an accusation. It's a door you hold open for them. If you've found the same silent pattern at work, [what to say when a coworker keeps asking for favors](/articles/coworker-keeps-asking-for-favors) covers it from the office side.

## What if I'm the one who has to remind them?

This is where many couples get stuck. If you assign a task and then check it, you're no longer partners. You're a manager and an employee, and managers always feel like they do more.

The fix is to give away a whole domain, not a task. Say they take Tuesday through Thursday dinners: the planning, the shopping, the cooking, the cleanup, and the decision of what happens when the chicken is gone. You don't remind. You don't redo it. If they serve cereal on a bad night, that is their call, and the family eats cereal. It will be messier than your way for a while. That mess is what it costs for you to stop being the one who holds the whole map in your head.

## Is this normal, or is something deeper wrong?

Feeling this way sometimes is very normal, and it comes in waves, often during a new baby, a job change, or a hard season. It's worth a closer look if the tally has gone silent and cold, if you've stopped telling them things because they "haven't earned it," or if you flinch when they offer help. Those are signs the problem has moved from chores to closeness. If you ever feel unsafe with your partner, please reach out for support. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is available at 1-800-799-7233.

Here's what I'd leave you with. Think about the last time you split a restaurant check and neither of you checked who ordered the extra side. For a moment, nobody was counting, because the table was one thing. Most couples don't need a perfect split. They need that feeling back, the sense that the cost belongs to the table and not to the person who happened to pick up the pen.
