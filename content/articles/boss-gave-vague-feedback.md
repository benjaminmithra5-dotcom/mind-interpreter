Your manager says, "I'd just like to see you be more strategic." You nod. You say, "Sure, that makes sense." Then you walk back to your desk with no idea what you're supposed to do differently on Thursday.

If you're wondering what to say when your boss gives vague feedback, the first thing to know is that the fuzziness usually isn't a trap. Often your manager has a feeling about your work and hasn't turned it into words yet. Your job, gently, is to help them do that. Here are some lines for each stage.

## What do I say in the moment?

The sting arrives first, and it makes you want to agree quickly so the conversation can end. Resist that. Give yourself one beat before you reply, and let your first words be a thank-you plus a request:

- "Thanks for telling me. I want to get this right. Can you think of a time in the last month when you wished I'd done it differently?"
- "If I were doing this well, what would you see me doing that you don't see now?"
- "Is there a meeting coming up where you'd like me to try it?"

Notice that each one asks for a scene, not a definition. A word like "strategic" has a hundred meanings, but a Tuesday meeting where you answered a question instead of asking one has only one. Scenes are easier for your boss to produce, and they give you something you can actually practice.

## How do I find out what they actually mean?

Say it back in your own, smaller words, and let them correct you. "So when you say 'speak up more,' do you mean offering an opinion before the group decides, or being more visible with the executives?" You're offering two guesses. They'll almost always say "Well, more the first one," and now the feedback has edges.

If they truly can't say, try, "What would make you notice the change?" It's a gentler way to ask, "How will I know I've fixed it?" A manager who can't define the problem can often still describe the relief.

## What if I'm caught off guard in a public setting?

Sometimes vague feedback lands in a team meeting or a chat thread, where you can't ask follow-up questions without an audience. You don't owe a performance. Keep it to one sentence: "Appreciate that. Can I grab ten minutes with you later this week so I can understand it better?" Then schedule it before the end of the day. Calm and brief beats clever here, and the real conversation can wait for a closed door. If the comment was sharp, a plain "Noted, thank you" buys you the evening to think.

## What do I write afterward?

Within a day, send a short note, three sentences at most. It does two jobs: it confirms you heard them correctly, and it quietly turns a feeling into a record.

"Thanks for the chat today. What I took away: you'd like me to offer a recommendation, not just options, in the Monday planning meeting. I'll try that starting next week. Tell me if I've misread it."

The last sentence matters. It invites a correction, so your boss can fix a misunderstanding in thirty seconds rather than in your next review. Notice, too, that you've named one specific behavior and one date. That's the whole trick. You don't have to solve "strategic." You have to choose one visible thing to try first.

## What if they keep dodging?

Some managers stay vague every time you ask. A few possibilities exist, and you don't need to decide which one is true. Maybe they're rushed. Maybe they're uncomfortable giving criticism. Maybe the unclear part is real: the expectations haven't been decided above them either.

Try narrowing the choice, since a yes-or-no is easier to answer than an essay: "Of everything on my plate, which one would you most like to see me handle differently?" And if you've asked twice and still can't get a picture, name what you've done: "I've tried to act on the last two pieces of feedback, and I'm worried I'm guessing. Could we pick one thing together for the next month?" That isn't confrontation. It's a request for a shared target.

## What about the feeling afterward?

Vague criticism is hard in a particular way. With nothing specific to argue with, your mind fills in the blank with the worst version. If you notice yourself running the conversation again on the drive home, [Can't Stop Replaying What You Said?](/articles/cant-stop-replaying-what-you-said) may help you tell a loop from a real problem.

One thing I'd offer: a vague comment is a small amount of information. "Be more strategic" tells you your manager wants something. It does not tell you that you're failing, and it certainly doesn't tell you who you are. Let the words shrink to their actual size, then go find out what's inside them. Next time it happens, try just this much: after the nod, before you leave the room, ask for one example. A single scene is enough to start with. Ask it kindly, before the door closes behind you. It's usually the difference between carrying a fog around all week and carrying a plan.
