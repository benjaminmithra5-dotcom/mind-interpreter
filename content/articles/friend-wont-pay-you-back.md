You're at dinner with the group, and the check comes. Your friend slides her card across without a word, and you do the quiet math you've done a dozen times since March. Four hundred dollars. She booked her flight, and she's posting from the beach this week. You say nothing and pass the pepper.

If you're searching for what to say when a friend won't pay you back, I suspect the money isn't the whole problem. It's the way a small unspoken number can sit between two people and slowly change how they talk.

## Which do you want back, the money or the friend?

Before you write anything, answer one question: if the money never came, would you be able to live with that?

Try this. Imagine you wrote the amount off today, the way you'd write off a gift. Notice what happens in your body. If there's relief, the debt may be costing you more than it's worth, and forgiving it might be the real fix. If there's a hot, sinking feeling, then you need that money, or you need to be treated as someone who matters, and either is a fine reason to ask.

Knowing which one you're after changes your tone. People who want the money ask clearly. People who want to be respected tend to hint, and hints are what get ignored.

## Why does the silence cost you more than the ask?

Because you're already paying. You've probably noticed yourself keeping track of what she buys. You may have stopped suggesting plans, or started answering her texts a beat slower than you used to. None of that is petty. It's what an open question does to a friendship when nobody will close it.

I'd also guess she feels it too. A person who owes money and says nothing is often not scheming. They're embarrassed, and embarrassment makes people disappear, or act extra cheerful, or avoid your name on their screen. Your ask may be a relief to both of you.

## What do I say the first time?

Say it by text, privately, in the morning. A text gives her time to react without an audience, and she can have her wince before she answers. Name the loan, say the awkwardness out loud, and ask one question.

> Hey, I've felt weird bringing this up, so I'm just going to be plain. I lent you $400 in March for the flight, and I'd like to get it settled. What payment would feel doable for you?

Notice the last line. It lets her choose the size of the first step, and people who feel cornered by a total can usually agree to a piece of it. If she says $50 a month, say "That works. Can you send the first one on the first?" Then it's a date on a calendar instead of a mood.

If you'd rather not type a speech, an app request does half the work. Send the exact amount with a note like "March flight." The request itself is the reminder, so you never have to say the sentence out loud.

## What if she goes quiet or says "soon"?

"Soon" is a word that costs nothing. Wait a week, then send one line: "Checking in on the $400. Can you do the first $50 by Friday?" A date gives her something to say yes or no to. A vague "let me know" gives her room to stay vague.

Leave out the beach photos, the new shoes, and anything that starts "I saw that you…" That sentence turns a question about money into a trial about her character, and she'll defend herself instead of paying you.

If a second message gets nothing, you've learned something, and it's okay to say so: "I'm not going to keep bringing this up. Here's where I am. I'd like to be paid back by the end of the year. If that can't happen, tell me, and we'll figure out what's fair." You've set a boundary without an accusation, and the next move belongs to her.

## When is it time to let it go?

When asking again would cost you more peace than the money is worth, or when you realize you need the friend more than the cash. Letting go works best when you say it, because forgiveness that stays secret turns into resentment.

> I've been carrying the $400 around, and I don't want it between us anymore. I'm going to consider it settled. I'd love to see you, though.

That's not weakness. You're choosing what the debt will be, instead of letting it choose for you. Be honest with yourself about one thing: say it only if you mean it. If you say "forget it" through your teeth, she'll hear the teeth.

## What do I say when she asks to borrow again?

This one catches people. Someone who owes you $400 sometimes asks for $60 more, and your mouth says yes before your mind has caught up.

Have a sentence ready: "I can't lend while the last one is still open. When that's cleared, ask me again." It's kind, it's true, and it doesn't call her a bad person. It just describes how your lending works.

And next time, before you hand anyone money, ask yourself what I call the goodbye test: if this never comes back, can I still be glad to see this person? If not, the answer is a gift of a smaller amount, or a kind no.
