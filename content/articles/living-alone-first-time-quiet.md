You carry the last box in, lock the door, and stand there with your keys still in your hand. Nobody asks what's for dinner. Nobody is in the next room. The refrigerator hums, and it is somehow the loudest thing you have ever heard.

If you're living alone for the first time and it's too quiet, I want to say first that the quiet is not a verdict on your choice. It's a gap where other people's noise used to be. And a gap can be filled, a little at a time. Here are the steps I'd take, in order.

## Why is the quiet so loud the first few weeks?

For years, your days came with a soundtrack you never chose: a roommate's shower, a partner's keys, a parent's television, a dorm hallway. You didn't notice it, the way you don't notice a clock until it stops. Now it has stopped, and your ears keep searching for it.

So the first thing to understand is that you aren't missing "company" in some large, vague way. You're missing particular sounds. That matters, because particular things are easier to replace than a vague feeling.

## Which hour is the hard one?

Don't try to fix the whole day. Over the next three evenings, notice the hour the quiet gets heavy. For many people it's not midnight. It's 6:15, right after you put down your bag, or Sunday around four, when the light turns gold and nothing is happening.

Then finish this sentence out loud: "At that hour, I used to hear ___." A door. A pan. Someone asking how the day was. Whatever you say is your target. You aren't trying to get that person back, only that sound, or the feeling of being expected.

## What can you do with the first ten minutes at home?

Your arrival is the loneliest minute of the day, because it used to be the most social one. So change it on purpose.

Don't drop onto the couch. Keep your coat on for a minute, open one window a few inches, and put on something with a voice in it before you take your shoes off. Say something aloud, even if it's only "Okay, I'm home." It feels silly the first night and a little less silly by the fifth. Then make the same first small thing every time, such as filling a glass of water or starting the kettle. A repeated start tells your body that this is a place where things begin, not just a place where the day goes to end.

## How do you put a little company back in the room?

Aim for the target you named. If it was the sound of someone cooking nearby, try a call with a sibling or a friend during the hour you'd both be at the stove. The rule is no agenda. Prop the phone on the counter, chop your onions, talk when you feel like it and be quiet when you don't. That's what a roommate is, mostly: someone being quiet near you.

If it was a voice, turn on a radio station instead of a playlist. A live host is better than anything you chose, because you can't skip them, and they sound like a person in the middle of a day.

Then use your whole place. It's easy to shrink down to the bed and a laptop. Eat at the table, read in the chair you bought and never sat in, and give each room a visit before bed. A home that's lived in from end to end sounds less empty than one you only use a corner of.

And once a week, put one real person on the calendar before the week begins, ideally somewhere other than your apartment. If the name that comes to mind is one you've drifted from, [this piece on texting an old friend after years](/articles/reconnect-with-old-friend-after-years) may help with the first message.

## What about the noises at night?

The creaks are real, and so is the way your mind explains them at 1 a.m. Before bed, do a closing-up round, the same way each night: lock, stove, window, a glance at the hallway, light off. Two minutes, always in that order. It's an old household habit, and its whole purpose is to give your mind a finished list so it stops compiling its own. After a couple of weeks, the creaks tend to turn into just the sounds your building makes.

## How long until it feels like home?

I won't give you a number, because I'd be making it up. What I can say is that the quiet usually changes character. At first it feels like an absence. Later, some evenings, it starts to feel like room. One night you'll catch yourself humming in the kitchen without having meant to.

Some evenings will still be hard, and that's allowed. A hard Sunday in week three doesn't mean the plan failed. It means it was Sunday.

If the loneliness stays heavy for weeks, say it out loud to a friend or a doctor, and don't carry it quietly alone. If you'd like to think it through with me, listening first, I'd be glad to [talk with you in a private conversation](/consultation).

If you're in crisis, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line right away (for example, 988 in the US).

Some night soon, you'll wash a single fork and set it in the drying rack, and it will look less like a loss and more like a table set exactly the way you like it.
