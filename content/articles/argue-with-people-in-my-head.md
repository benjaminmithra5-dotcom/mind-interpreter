You're rinsing shampoo out of your hair, and you're in the middle of a speech. Your sister said something at lunch about how you "always" skip the holidays, and now you have the answer. Actually you have three answers, each sharper than the last. Your jaw is tight. Then the water runs cold and you remember she isn't here. She isn't saying any of this today.

If you've ever wondered why you argue with people in your head, you're in very common company. I don't think it's a flaw, and I don't think it means something is wrong with you. I think it's a mind trying to finish something it couldn't finish out loud.

## Why does the other person always lose?

Notice who's in that argument. You wrote every line. The other person says exactly the unfair thing you were afraid of, in exactly the tone that makes you angriest, and then you flatten them. It feels like a fight. It's closer to a play where you wrote both parts and gave yourself the better one.

That's the first thing worth seeing. The person in your head is a character, assembled from the worst thing they ever said and the worst thing you fear they think. Your real sister might say something much softer, or something more annoying, or something that surprises you. The character can't do that. It only says what you've already prepared for.

So when the argument ends in a victory and you still feel shaky, that isn't strange. You beat someone who wasn't there, and some part of you knows it.

## What is the argument really trying to say?

Here's something to try the next time it starts. Let it run to the end, then ask what the last line was. Not the cleverest line. The one you keep arriving at, the one your voice drops on when the speech is over.

Picture twenty minutes of rehearsed rebuttals that turn out to be holding up one small, plain sentence, like "You never asked how I was doing." Yours might be "I did a lot for this family," or "You don't get to decide that for me," or "I wanted you to be proud."

That sentence is the message. The rest is armor. Once you find it, you have a choice you didn't have when it was a tangle: say it to the real person, or keep it for yourself and let the armor go. Either is fine. Only the second one leaves you arguing in the dark.

## Is there a real conversation hiding in there?

Sometimes the argument is a rehearsal for a talk you know you need to have, and your mind is practicing because nobody has set a date. If that's true, I'd suggest rehearsing less, and in a smaller way.

Prepare only the first thirty seconds. Something like: "There's something from lunch I'd like to understand better, and I'm not trying to win." Then stop. You can't script what comes next, because you aren't the one who writes it, and every minute spent writing their lines is a minute spent fighting the character instead of the person.

If there's no real conversation to have, because the person is gone, or the moment passed years ago, say so to yourself plainly: "This one isn't going to happen." That's a sad sentence. It's also the one that lets the rehearsals stop.

## What if you ask a question you can't answer?

The imaginary argument runs on certainty. You already know what they'll say, so you never have to wonder.

Try breaking that. Write down one question about the other person that you honestly don't know the answer to. Not a trap, like "Why are you so selfish?" A real one: "What was lunch like for you?" or "Did you know that comment would land that hard?" Then see how the argument feels with that question sitting next to it. It tends to go quiet, because the character can't answer a question that you can't answer yourself.

If you can, ask it out loud, to the real person, someday. If you can't, the question still does its work by reminding you that they are someone with an inside you can't see, and not only the one who wounded you.

## Why does it happen in the shower and the car?

Look at where it happens. In the shower, on a long drive, folding laundry, lying in bed. These are the places where your hands are busy and your attention is free, so it goes looking for unfinished business, and the argument is usually first in line.

I wouldn't try to fight that. I'd give those moments something else to hold. Pick one thing for the shower, say, a song you have to get all the way through, with the words. Or in the car, a podcast about something with no people in it at all, like bridges or birds. The argument can wait outside. It will often still be there at the end of the song, and sometimes it has lost interest.

If this has been constant and affecting your sleep or daily life for weeks, it's worth talking to a doctor or mental health professional.

And if you'd like to say the real thing out loud to someone who will just listen first, I'd be glad to [talk with you in a private conversation](/consultation). Arguments that replay something you already said are a slightly different knot, and I wrote about those in [can't stop replaying what you said](/articles/cant-stop-replaying-what-you-said).

The version of your sister in the shower has never once paused and said, "You know, you're right, I'm sorry." Only the real one can say that. It's a long shot, and it's the only one that counts.
