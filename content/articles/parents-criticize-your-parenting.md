Your daughter drops a cookie on the floor, you tell her it's the last one for today, and your mother says, "Oh, let her have it. You were never this strict with yourself." Everyone at the table hears it. Your daughter looks at you, then at her grandmother, then at the cookie. You feel about twelve years old.

If you're searching for what to say when your parents criticize your parenting, I'd guess the hard part isn't the words. It's that the comment lands on two things at once: the decision, and the child you used to be. With the holidays coming, there will be more tables like this one. Here are lines you can keep in your pocket.

## What do I say in the moment, in front of the kids?

Say less than you want to. A short, level sentence lets the room move on, and it shows your child that the adult in charge is calm.

Try one of these, and pick the one that sounds like your own voice:

- "That's how we do it at our house."
- "I hear you. We're going to stick with it today."
- "Noted. Pass the potatoes?"

Notice that none of them argue, and none of them ask permission. And if the comment was aimed at the child ("Grandma says you can have it"), speak to the child, not over your mother: "Grandma sees it differently. At our house, today the answer is no." That tells your kid the rule still stands without making her grandmother the villain.

## Why does it hurt so much when it comes from my own parents?

Because no one else has watched you for your whole life. A coworker's opinion about your child is an opinion. Your father's opinion is a ruling from the first judge you ever had.

It may help to know that grandparents often say these things from a place that isn't about you at all. Some are scared that the way they raised you is being called wrong. Some feel pushed to the edge of the family and are trying to find a seat. A comment like "we never did it that way" is sometimes a plain request to be told they did okay.

You don't have to say they did everything right. But you can say one true thing: "You did a lot of things well, and I'm doing some things differently. Both can be true." You may be surprised how often the criticism softens when the person feels seen.

## What if they say "we did it that way and you turned out fine"?

This is the sentence that makes people want to scream, so plan for it. Don't litigate the past. Pick the part of it that's true and kind, then return to today: "I did turn out fine, thanks to you. And what we know about this has changed, so we're trying it this way."

Keep it about the information, not about her mistakes. "Advice changed" lets her keep her dignity, and dignity is usually what the argument was protecting.

## What should I say later, when we're alone?

The moment is for short lines. The real talk belongs somewhere private, another day, ideally on a walk or a phone call when no one is tired or hungry. Start with what you want, not with what she did wrong:

"I want you in my kids' lives, and I want it to be easy for all of us. When you correct me in front of them, it makes me defensive and it confuses them. If you're worried about something, tell me at the sink or call me, and I'll listen to it."

That gives her somewhere to put the worry, which is better than asking her to stop worrying. Then ask: "What's the thing you're most afraid will go wrong?" Often it's one specific fear, like the cold or the sugar. You can answer that one fear directly ("She has a coat in the car, I promise") and watch ten smaller comments disappear.

## How do I hold the line without a fight?

Choose your two. Before the visit, decide the two rules you won't bend on, maybe bedtime and what's said about your kid's body, and let everything else go. You'll sound calm about the little stuff, which gives your firm answers more weight.

Agree on a signal with your partner, such as a hand on the shoulder or the word "refill?", that means "I'll take this one" or "I need a minute." You don't need to ambush anyone. You just don't want to be alone at the table.

If a comment keeps coming back after you've asked once, follow through gently: "I asked you not to say that in front of her. We're going to step outside for a few minutes." Then do it. Most people believe a boundary only after they've seen it happen once.
