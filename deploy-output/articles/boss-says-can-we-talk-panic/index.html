When Your Boss Says 'Can We Talk?' and You Panic

16 November 2026 · 5 min read

The message pops up at 2:47 on a Tuesday. "Can we talk? Come by when you have a sec." Your coffee is still warm, and you can no longer taste it. You read the message again, looking for a smile in it, and there isn't one. There's nothing in it at all.

If you panic when your boss says "can we talk," you're not being dramatic. You're reacting to a message built to make people nervous, because it carries almost no information. Let's take the questions one at a time.

Why does "can we talk?" hit so hard?

Because it's a blank, and a worried mind hates a blank. Three words give you no topic, no tone and no clue how big it is. So your brain does what it always does with missing information: it fills the gap with the worst thing it can imagine. Your heart speeds up before you've had a single actual thought.

Notice what you're reacting to. It isn't your boss's voice or face, only a short string of letters on a screen. You're reading a mood into words that don't have one.

Here's a small thing that helps. Write down the scary story, then write three dull ones beside it. "She needs my signature." "He wants to move Thursday's meeting." "She's confused by a spreadsheet." Dull reasons are far more common than dramatic ones, and putting them on paper next to the scary one stops it from being the only story in the room.

What do I do in the minutes before the meeting?

Give your hands a job. Pick up a notebook and write the date at the top, then one question you'd like to ask yourself. Maybe "what's the status of the vendor thing?" or "can I get Friday afternoon off?" It sounds minor, but it changes your role. You're walking in as someone with something to say, not as someone waiting for a verdict.

Then take the long way. If the office is down a hall, go past the window instead of straight through the open floor, and look at something far away for a few steps. If you're remote, close the laptop lid halfway and stand up for the two minutes before the call. Movement spends some of the nervous energy that would otherwise leave you rigid in the chair.

When you reply, resist "Sure, what's up?" because it invites the worst answer. Try: "On my way. Anything I should bring?" It sounds like a cooperative person, and it gives your manager an easy chance to say, "Nope, just a quick one."

Can I ask what it's about?

Yes, though how you ask matters. Asking "Is this bad?" puts your manager on the spot. Asking "Should I look something up before I come?" gets the same information without making anyone defend themselves.

There's a second, longer fix, and it belongs in a calm moment, not in the panic. Some day when nothing is wrong, you could say: "When I get a message that just says 'can we talk,' my mind goes straight to the worst. If it's routine, could you add a few words, like 'quick question' or 'nothing urgent'?" Most managers have no idea how their short messages land. They typed four words between meetings. A small request, made kindly, usually costs them nothing to grant. (If yours gets irritated by it, that tells you something about the workplace, not about you.)

What should I say when I sit down?

Skip the nervous small talk about the weather. Right at the start, ask: "Before we get into it, is this a heads-up, a question, or something I should write down?"

That one sentence sorts the conversation into a category, and your body relaxes when it knows what kind of thing it's facing. A heads-up means listen. A question means answer. "Write it down" means you've been given something serious, and you can pick up the pen with no surprise left to manage.

It also shows something about you: you're steady enough to ask for the shape of a conversation. That impression lasts longer than any nervousness did.

How do I stop the dread from stacking up?

I think the dread feeds on a record that's only half kept. You remember the one time the meeting went badly, and you forget the nine that were about parking passes and calendar invites.

So keep the other half. After each of these meetings, spend ten seconds on a note in your phone with two columns of a sentence each: what I feared, and what it was. "Feared: a complaint. Was: new password policy." After a month or two, read it back. You're not trying to talk yourself out of a feeling. You're giving it real data, and feelings tend to loosen their grip when the numbers stop agreeing with them.

If this has been constant and affecting your sleep or daily life for weeks, it's worth talking to a doctor or mental health professional.

What if it really is bad news?

Sometimes it is. A reorganization, a missed target, a tough piece of feedback. The panic wasn't foolish, only early.

If that happens, you don't have to respond well in the room. Ask for two things: "Can you tell me what happens next, and when?" and "I'd like to take this in properly. Can I come back to you tomorrow morning?" Those two sentences are enough. You don't have to argue, agree or decide anything with your heart still racing.
