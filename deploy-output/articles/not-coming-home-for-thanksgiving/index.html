Tell Your Parents You're Not Coming Home for Thanksgiving

21 October 2026 · 5 min read

Your mother's name lights up the screen, and you let it ring out again. You know what she'll ask. Have you booked your flight yet? And you still haven't told her that you won't be on it.

If you're trying to work out how to tell your parents you're not coming home for Thanksgiving, you probably have a real reason: money, a work schedule, a baby who won't sleep on planes, or a year that left you with nothing in the tank. The reason matters less than you'd think. What matters is how the news arrives. Here is the order I'd do it in.

Pick the date you'll tell them

Thanksgiving falls on November 26 this year, so aim to tell them in the first week of November. Waiting doesn't soften the news. It lets your parents plan a menu, buy the bigger turkey and mention you to the neighbors. A parent who hears four weeks out has time to be sad and then recover. One who hears four days out only has time to be hurt.

If you have a brother or sister, tell them the day before. Otherwise they hear it from Mom as a news bulletin, and they may end up as the negotiator you never asked for.

Write one sentence and one yes

Put two lines on a sticky note. The first is your no: "I'm not going to make it home for Thanksgiving this year." The second is your yes: "But I'm coming the weekend of December 12," or "I'll call at three on Thursday while you're cooking."

A no with nothing attached sounds like a door closing. A no with a date sounds like a rescheduling. Make the yes something you will actually do, with a day on it. "Soon" doesn't count.

Keep the reason to one honest line. A long explanation hands your parents a list of problems to solve, and the next thing you hear is "Oh, we'll buy the ticket."

Say the decision in the first twenty seconds

Call. Don't text. If one parent will take it harder, call that one, or put them both on speaker. Open with: "I'm calling because I've made a decision about Thanksgiving, and I wanted you to hear it from me." Then say your sentence, then your yes, then stop.

Notice what not to say: "I might not be able to," "I'm still figuring it out," "we'll see." A maybe sounds like a door left ajar, and people push on doors left ajar. Then comes the silence on the line. It will feel very long. Let it be long.

Know the guilt lines before they arrive

Most parents reach for one of three sentences. You can have an answer ready for each.

  • "We won't be here forever." Try: "I know. That's why I'm planning December now."
  • "Your cousin is flying in from Denver." Try: "I'm glad she is. I still can't this year."
  • "Did we do something?" If the answer is no, say it plainly: "No. This is about my calendar, not about you."

After that, you don't need new arguments for each round. Say the same calm sentence in the same words: "I know it's disappointing. I'm still not coming this year." Repetition isn't rude. It tells them the matter is settled, and settled things stop being fought over.

If your mother cries, you can stay on the line without changing the answer. "I hate that this makes you sad. I'm not changing my mind, and I'm still here." You are also allowed to end the call kindly: "Let's talk tomorrow, once it's had a minute to sink in."

Give them a piece of the day

What a parent grieves is rarely the dinner. It's the picture: every chair filled, the noise, the phone camera held up for the one photo of the year. So ask them, honestly, "What's the part of Thursday you'd miss me at most?" The answer may surprise you. It might be grace, or the pie, or arguing over the football game.

Then offer to be in that piece. Ask your dad to call you in during the stuffing. Make her sweet potato recipe in your own kitchen with her on video, both of you stirring. Ask to be on speaker when the table goes around saying what they're thankful for. A small, specific appearance often does more than a long apology.

Plan your own Thursday before it arrives

The hardest hour is rarely the phone call. It's around three o'clock on the day itself, when your kitchen is quiet and your family's group chat is full of photos.

Plan that hour in advance. Say yes to the coworker's invitation, take a volunteer shift, or host a Friendsgiving where everyone brings one dish. If you're staying home by choice, give the day a shape: cook one thing from your family's table, take a long walk before dark, and save a movie for the evening. When the guilt wave comes, and it likely will, let it pass through, then send your mom a photo of your plate.

If this ever turns into more than a wave and the day feels darker than sad, please don't sit in it by yourself.

If you're in crisis, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line right away (for example, 988 in the US).
