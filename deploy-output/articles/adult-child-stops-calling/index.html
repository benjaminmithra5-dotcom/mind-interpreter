What to Say When Your Adult Child Stops Calling

9 October 2026 · 6 min read

It's Sunday afternoon, and you're holding the phone again. You scroll to their name, look at the last text, which was three weeks ago, and put the phone back on the counter. You don't want to be the parent who calls too much. You also don't want to be the parent who gave up.

If you're trying to work out what to say when your adult child stops calling, I want to start with something that may loosen your chest a little. The silence is almost never as complete as it feels from your side. A grown child can go quiet for a hundred reasons: a heavy stretch at work, a rough patch they're ashamed of, a hurt they haven't found words for, or simply a life that got loud. Some of those reasons involve you. Many don't. You can't tell which one you're in yet, and that's okay. Your first message doesn't need to solve it. It only needs to be easy to receive.

What should the first message say?

I think the best first message does three things. It says you've been thinking of them, it asks for nothing, and it makes clear they don't owe you an answer today.

Most of us do the opposite. We write "Haven't heard from you in a while, is everything okay?" and it lands like a small bill. The reader now has to explain the silence, reassure you, and apologize, all before they've had coffee. Compare that with a message that holds the door open without asking anyone to walk through it.

Here are a few you can borrow and bend into your own voice:

  • "Saw a dog on my walk that looked exactly like the one we had when you were ten. Made me smile. No need to reply."
  • "I've been thinking about you this week. I'm not looking for anything. I just wanted you to know I'm here."
  • "I miss your voice. Whenever you feel like a call, any day, any hour, I'll pick up."

Notice that each one gives them something small and warm, and each ends by lifting the pressure. The first one works especially well if you can think of a true memory. A real detail from their life tells them you remember them as a person, not as a problem you're managing.

What if I did something wrong?

Maybe you already have a guess. A comment at the last holiday, a loan, a question about their partner, advice they didn't ask for. If you suspect your part, you can say so once, plainly, without a long defense.

"I think I may have hurt you when I said what I did about the move. I'm sorry. I'd like to hear how it landed, whenever you're ready, and I promise to just listen."

Then stop. Don't explain why you said it. Don't add "but I was only trying to help." An apology followed by a "but" gets read as only the "but." I've come to believe that the hardest part of repairing anything with a grown child is letting their version of events be true even when yours is too. If you find yourself replaying what you said at that dinner, I wrote about that in Can't Stop Replaying What You Said? Here's What to Do, and some of it may help here.

If you honestly have no idea what you did, it's fine to say that too: "I don't know if I did something, and I don't want to guess. If there's anything you want me to know, I'm listening."

Why is it so hard when they finally answer?

Sometimes the reply is short. "Hey. Busy. Talk soon." You read it six times, searching for the temperature of each word.

Try to answer the size of the message you were given. A two-word text deserves a two-line reply, not a paragraph. "Thanks for answering. Talk whenever." Then wait. If they've sent something cool, resist the urge to ask what's wrong or whether they're angry with you. Questions like that ask them to do emotional work they may not have room for yet.

If they come back with something sharper, like "I need space," take it at its word. You can say, "Okay. I hear you. I love you, and the door is open." That sentence is hard to write, because everything in you wants to ask for how long. Don't. Asking for a date turns a boundary into a negotiation, and I think a boundary that gets respected the first time leaves more room for them to come back.

How often is too often?

There's no magic number, but there is a useful test. Before you send anything, ask yourself, "If they never answer this, can I still send the next one without resentment?" If the honest answer is no, the message is secretly a demand, and it's better to wait a few days until it isn't.

One approach is to choose a rhythm you could keep up for a year, such as one small note every two or three weeks, and let that be the whole plan. A photo of the garden. A line from a song. The score of a game they used to follow. It sounds almost too small to matter. But a steady, undemanding thread tells a person the door isn't locked from your side, and that can be exactly what makes it possible to walk back through.

What do I do with my own days in the meantime?

I'd suggest giving the waiting some company. Put one thing in your week that has nothing to do with them and isn't up for negotiation: a Thursday morning walking group, a class, a standing coffee with a friend. It sounds small, but waiting swells to fill empty space, and a few fixed points in the week stop it from taking over every afternoon.

And tell one person, out loud, what's happening. Not to get advice. Just so you aren't carrying it silently into every dinner. If you'd like a space to say it all and hear yourself think, you're welcome to set up a private conversation with me.

If the waiting has started to affect your sleep, appetite or mood for weeks, it's worth talking to a doctor or mental health professional.

And if you have a real reason to think your child may be unsafe, don't wait for a reply. Contact your local authorities and ask for a welfare check.

If you're in crisis, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line right away (for example, 988 in the US).

Think of a time you were the one who didn't write back, not because you stopped caring, but because the longer you waited, the heavier the apology got. Your son or daughter may be standing in that same doorway right now, wanting to come in and not knowing what to say. The kindest thing you can do is leave a light on that asks nothing of them.
